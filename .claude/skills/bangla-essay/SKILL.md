# Bangla essays

The essays on this site are the author's prose, written in Bangla. The first set (the five
trellis essays, 2026-10-09) were rejected because they did not sound like him: too many dashes,
sentences with a word missing, telegraphic lists, prose that talked about the grid instead of
the world. This skill holds what he asked for instead. **His words are the standard; when in
doubt, re-read §1.**

## 1 · What the author asked for (his rules, near verbatim)

- *"I write in a continuous lyrical form."* The essay flows. When someone reads it aloud it
  should feel like a lyrical thing flowing like a poem.
- *"Each sentence has to make sense, has to be complete."* Not one sentence may read as though
  a word is missing.
- *"Every single sentence fits within the whole."* No sentence is a stray observation; each
  one carries the thought forward from the one before into the one after.
- *"There should not be a single long dash in an essay."* **No dashes at all** (— – ― or a
  spaced hyphen). Not one.
- *"I do not want semicolon that much, semicolon and colon that much."* Colons and semicolons
  are rare: a handful across a whole essay, and only where nothing else will do.
- *"There is no word limit … to contract a sentence to fill it with a dense meaning. Be as
  lyrical as you can. And you can make it longer if needed."* Never compress. Length is free.
- *"You can have long sentences … but those long sentences have to be lyrically coherent."*
  A long sentence is welcome when it breathes, when its clauses arrive in the order the
  thought unfolds and it lands cleanly at the end.
- *"Now you're becoming too lyrical and losing the philosophical depth. I want a perfect
  balance between poetry and philosophy … lyrical fluency and philosophical depth must merge
  together. There must be analysis, diagnosis, critical thought processing, everything as
  well, but all of it within the lyrical structure. The essays are kind of like poetry plus
  philosophy."* See §2a: this is the heart of the form.
- *"You are just going on and on telling stories, but lyricism has nothing to do with stories.
  Lyricism is a style, not a format of storytelling. Keep the philosophical analysis, clear
  answers, clear questions, clear step-by-step formulation. Some stories, some lyricism, some
  analysis, some really in-depth investigations and real discoveries … the essay has to be
  diverse, must not be monotonic. Each essay must suit its own trellis and grow like a
  trellis grows: as it rises from the ground it needs a lot of support. Philosophy, poetry,
  lyricism, stories, Socratic dialogues, depth: these are the supports through which the
  trellis is rising, and the essay follows that growth, in an Aristotelian sense."* See §2b.
- Language: modern Bangla that sounds comfortable **in Dhaka** (পানি not জল, গোসল not স্নান;
  no sadhu, no Sanskrit-heavy diction, nothing that reads as translated). **God is খোদা.**
  See memory `bangla-register`.

## 2 · The voice, concretely

- **Sentences, not fragments.** Every sentence has its subject and its finite verb, said or
  plainly understood. No sentence made of a noun phrase alone, no "X থেকে, Y হয়ে, Z-তে।"
  chains whose verb never comes.
- **Join with Bangla, not punctuation.** Where the first draft reached for a dash or a colon,
  the author would write a word: আর, কিন্তু, তবু, অথচ, যেন, যেমন, কারণ, তাই, যখন … তখন,
  যে … সে, এমনভাবে যে, আর সেই কারণেই. A comma and a connective, or a full stop and a new
  sentence that picks up the last one's word.
- **Images before abstractions.** Begin from the thing seen: the কাশবন, the train's smoke, the
  olive grove, the lamp in the cowshed. Let the idea rise out of the image in the same
  sentence or the next. An abstraction that never touches an image is the essay going dry.
- **Music.** Read each paragraph aloud in your head. Vary the length: a long, unfolding
  sentence, then a short one that settles it. Let a word or image return across paragraphs as
  a refrain. Cadence at paragraph ends matters most; end on a full, resonant sentence, never
  on a list.
- **No lists in disguise.** A sentence that names seven films or seven concepts in a row,
  separated by commas, is a table, not prose. If several things must be gathered, give each
  its own clause with its own verb, or choose the two that matter.
- **No jargon from the apparatus.** Do not talk about "কলাম", "সারি", "ঘর", "কোনাকুনি রেখা",
  "মাচা" as mechanics, and do not use a column label as if it were a word the reader already
  knows (বাজি, প্রহরা, ভাঙন as bare tags). Say what it means in plain words: "নিজের সবচেয়ে
  দামি জিনিসটা বাজি ধরে", "যে মরছে তার পাশে জেগে বসে থাকে". The grid is where the thinking
  came from; the essay speaks of the films, the people, the ideas and life.
- **Complete for a reader who has not opened the grid.** Every reference (a character, a
  scene, a term) is introduced so that a reader meeting it for the first time understands it.
- **Register.** Literate, warm, standard চলিত, the prose of a Bangladeshi writer at his desk.
  No academic hedging ("অনেক গবেষকের মতে" once is fine; a habit of it is not). No
  English-shaped constructions ("এটা লক্ষণীয় যে", "যা বোঝায় তা হলো").
- **Names and terms.** Bangla script; a foreign term in italics only when it is the point,
  and then explained in the same sentence. No Latin letters, no brackets, Bangla numerals.

## 2a · Poetry plus philosophy — the balance

Lyricism alone was the second failure (2026-10-09): beautiful, flowing, and thin. The essay
must **think**, and think hard, while it sings. Every paragraph does real intellectual work:

- **A claim.** Each paragraph advances a definite proposition about the work or the question,
  one a thoughtful reader could dispute. An image that does not carry a claim is decoration.
- **Analysis.** Take the claim apart: what exactly is the mechanism, the structure, the
  distinction? Name the difference between two things that look alike (two kinds of oneness,
  two kinds of seeing, two kinds of silence), and show why it matters.
- **Diagnosis.** Say what is going on underneath: why a character, a thinker, a whole oeuvre
  keeps returning to the same wound or question; what an idea is a symptom or an answer of.
- **Critical thought.** Argue. Test the claim against the strongest objection or the case
  that breaks it, concede what must be conceded, and say where an artist or thinker fails,
  contradicts himself, or leaves something unresolved. Compare and judge; do not only admire.
- **Consequence.** End a movement of thought on what follows from it, for the work, for the
  question, for how we live.

And all of it **inside the lyrical structure**: the argument is carried by the same flowing,
complete, read-aloud sentences, and the images are where the thinking happens, not ornaments
beside it. The test for each paragraph: read aloud, does it move like music; summarised in
one sentence, does it say something true, precise and non-obvious? Both answers must be yes.
A paragraph that passes only the first is too lyrical; only the second, too dry.

## 2b · The shape — an essay that grows like a trellis

**Lyricism is the voice, never the format.** Plot retelling is not lyricism and not thought.
Tell a story only as evidence, briefly, the one scene that carries the point, and then think
about it. If a paragraph is mostly "and then she… and then he…", it has failed.

**Diversity of modes.** A good essay moves between several kinds of writing, each chosen
because the thought at that point needs it, never the same mode for long:

- **The question, posed plainly.** Name the essay's real question early, in one clear
  sentence a reader can hold. Later questions sharpen it.
- **The clear answer.** When the essay reaches an answer, state it plainly, then defend it.
- **Step-by-step formulation.** Build an argument in visible steps inside prose: first this,
  therefore this, but then this follows (প্রথমত … দ্বিতীয়ত … তাহলে …), so the reader can check each
  link. No bullet lists; the steps live in sentences.
- **A Socratic exchange.** Where an idea needs testing, let two voices question each other in
  short alternating paragraphs, each line in quotation marks ‘ ’ (never dash-led dialogue):
  an objector and the essay, or two of the trellis's own figures. A few exchanges, not a play.
- **Investigation.** Go deep into one cell, one scene, one concept, and dig until something
  is found that was not visible at the start.
- **Discovery.** The essay must arrive at something real: a pattern no single film or
  thinker shows, a distinction that dissolves a confusion, a reason that explains a recurrence.
  Say it as a discovery, plainly, at the moment it lands.
- **Story,** brief, as evidence. **Lyric passages,** where feeling and image carry the
  insight further than argument can.

**Grow like a trellis, toward an end.** The essay is organic in Aristotle's sense: it starts
from its ground (the concrete thing, one image or scene from its trellis), each stage grows
out of the one before it and needs it, the supports above are what let it rise, and it
reaches toward its *telos*, the understanding it was always growing toward, which closes it.
No two essays should have the same shape: the shape grows from what *this* trellis is. A
trellis of films grows differently from one of thinkers; a trellis about the self differs
from one about God.

## 4 · The trellis essay specifically

- File `public/trellises/<name>/essay.md`: a `# Title` line, then **one** intro sentence (it
  renders as the subtitle), then paragraphs separated by blank lines. No subheadings, lists,
  bullets or bold. `trellis.js` `renderEssay()` reads exactly this.
- **Subject:** the deep understanding that comes *out of* the grid, what no single film or
  cell shows: what travels across a whole career, where the pattern breaks and why, the
  unexpected cell that changes the reading of the rest. Not matrix algebra, not a summary of
  cells, not an introduction to the person.
- **Cell links:** `[words](#RC)` (row R, column C, both 0–6), `[words](#1-RC)` /
  `[words](#2-RC)` in খোদা (দার্শনিক / নবি). They render as links that open the cell over
  the essay, with the small cell number after them. **The bracketed words must be a natural
  part of a complete sentence**, so the sentence reads perfectly if the link were plain text.
  Never a link followed by a dash or colon as a caption. Six to twelve per essay.
- Read the whole `trellis.md` first (every cell and synthesis): the essay must be true to it.
- Length is free. The first set ran ~1,500–2,200 words; longer is welcome when it flows.

## 5 · Process

1. Read §1–§3, the trellis, and (for ideas only, never for sentences) any earlier essay.
2. Find the essay's one governing image or thought, the thing it will return to at the end.
3. Write paragraph by paragraph, each one opening from the last one's closing thought.
4. **Read-aloud pass, sentence by sentence.** For each sentence ask: does it have its verb?
   Would a listener understand it without seeing the grid? Is any word missing? Does it follow
   from the sentence before? Does it sound like speech, or like notes? Fix every failure;
   expect to rewrite a third of the draft here.
5. Run the checker until it reports **0 errors**, then read every WARN and resolve it:
   `python3 -I .claude/skills/bangla-essay/scripts/check_essay.py <essay.md>`
6. When an agent wrote it, a **second, fresh agent** does step 4 and 5 again cold, briefed by
   `references/cold-reader.md`. The writer is blind to its own gaps; the cold reader is not.
